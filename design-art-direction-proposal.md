## Design/Art Direction Proposal with Moodboard — Best Practices

### 1. Structure Your Proposal Clearly

A strong art direction proposal typically follows this flow:

**Discovery → Direction → Moodboard → Application**

- **Creative Brief Summary** — restating the problem, audience, and goals
- **Art Direction Concept** — a named direction with a tagline (e.g. *"Calm Authority"*, *"Playful Precision"*)
- **Visual Principles** — 3–5 adjectives that guide every decision
- **Moodboard** — the emotional proof
- **Applied Concepts** — showing how the direction translates to UI

---

### 2. Building the Moodboard

**Source diverse references, not just UI:**

- Photography & film stills (mood, lighting, color)
- Physical textures & materials
- Typography specimens
- Fine art or illustration
- Competitor & adjacent industry UI
- Motion/video references (if relevant)

**Organize by visual layer:**

| Layer | What to show |
|---|---|
| Color | Palette swatches, photography with dominant tones |
| Typography | Type pairings, editorial layouts |
| Imagery style | Photography direction, illustration style |
| Texture/depth | Surface materials, shadows, grain |
| Motion | Stills from animations, transition references |

**Tools:** Figma (most common), Milanote, Cosmos, Pinterest, or a simple PDF grid.

---

### 3. Presenting Multiple Directions

Always present **2–3 distinct directions**, not just one. This:

- Gives stakeholders agency
- Surfaces hidden preferences
- Shows your range

Label them clearly:

- **Direction A** — Conservative / Safe / On-brand
- **Direction B** — Aspirational / Stretch
- **Direction C** — Disruptive / Bold

---

### 4. Annotate, Don't Just Show

Every moodboard should have **callouts explaining *why*** each reference was included:

> *"This hotel lobby photo — the warm indirect lighting and generous whitespace translate to low-density UI with ample padding and soft shadows."*

Avoid silent grids. Stakeholders read intent into images differently than designers do.

---

### 5. Bridge to UI Explicitly

After the moodboard, show **direct translation** to design decisions:

| Moodboard feeling | UI decision |
|---|---|
| Warm, editorial photography | Sepia-tinted image overlays |
| Sharp geometric art | Icon style, corner radius = 0 |
| Luxury materials (stone, linen) | Neutral palette, high contrast text |
| Energetic, layered collage | Overlapping cards, bold type |

---

### 6. Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Too many images** — 12–20 is plenty; beyond that it loses focus
- **All UI references** — you'll end up copying, not creating
- **No narrative** — a moodboard without a concept statement is just a Pinterest board
- **Skipping the "why"** — annotations are what make it a proposal, not a presentation
- **One direction only** — removes client collaboration from the process

---

### 7. Deliverable Format Tips

- **Figma** — best for collaborative review and easy iteration
- **PDF** — good for async stakeholders and formal sign-off
- **Loom/video walkthrough** — highly effective for remote teams; narrate the intent
- Keep it **one page per direction** if possible — forces you to prioritize

---

The core principle: a moodboard isn't decoration — it's a **design argument**. Every image should be intentional evidence for your creative direction.
